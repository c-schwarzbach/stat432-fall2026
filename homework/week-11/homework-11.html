(b) General K-means function

The function below accepts an n × p data matrix, any valid K, initial nonempty cluster assignments, and a maximum iteration count. At each iteration it recomputes the means and assigns observations to the nearest mean. Empty clusters retain their previous center for the next assignment step. The returned means are recomputed from the final assignments, and the objective is the within-cluster sum of squared Euclidean distances (WCSS).

import numpy as np

def cluster_means(X, labels, K, previous=None):
    centers = np.empty((K, X.shape[1]))
    for k in range(K):
        if np.any(labels == k):
            centers[k] = X[labels == k].mean(axis=0)
        else:
            centers[k] = previous[k] if previous is not None else X[k]
    return centers

def my_kmeans(X, K, initial_labels, max_iter=100):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    labels = np.asarray(initial_labels, dtype=int).copy()  # 0,...,K-1
    assert X.ndim == 2 and len(labels) == len(X)
    assert set(labels) == set(range(K))  # nonempty initial clusters
    for iteration in range(max_iter):
        centers = cluster_means(X, labels, K)
        distance_squared = ((X[:, None, :] - centers[None, :, :])**2).sum(axis=2)
        updated = np.argmin(distance_squared, axis=1)
        if np.array_equal(updated, labels):
            break
        labels = updated
    centers = cluster_means(X, labels, K, previous=centers)
    wcss = float(((X - centers[labels])**2).sum())
    return labels, centers, wcss

(c) Preferred number of colors

I would choose K = 10 for this image. The WCSS falls from about 70.5 million at K = 2 to 38.1 million at K = 3 and 12.9 million at K = 5, and then drops by a further factor of more than 12 to about 1.03 million at K = 10 (about 96 per pixel, i.e. a typical color error of roughly 10 RGB units). The reconstructions show why: K = 5 merges the red circle into the orange family, merges the two sky tones into one, folds the trunk and path into the green field, and loses the sun’s yellow, while K = 10 reproduces every distinct region of the picture (sun, snow caps, both mountains, red and orange circles, trunk and path, field and two sky tones). This image is made of roughly ten flat colors, so K = 10 gives a near-exact fit; for a photograph with a smooth range of colors the elbow would typically come earlier. Because K-means is run from random starts, the lowest-WCSS result over many starts should be used when comparing different K.

Question 3 — Hierarchical clustering of cars

(a) Standardization and three linkage methods

The provided mtcars.csv contains 32 cars and 11 numeric variables. I excluded the car name column from the distance calculation and used it only for dendrogram labels. Each numeric variable was transformed using (value − sample mean) / sample standard deviation. I then computed Euclidean distances and fit single, complete and average linkage hierarchical clustering.

Comparison: Single linkage joins groups according to their nearest pair and can exhibit chaining, adding individual cars to a long cluster. Complete linkage uses the farthest pair, generally favoring tighter, more compact groups. Average linkage uses mean pairwise distances and often produces an intermediate compromise. The dendrogram branch heights show dissimilarities at which cars or groups merge, and their precise cluster compositions differ by linkage method.

(b) Selected solution: complete linkage, 3 clusters

I chose complete linkage with three clusters to obtain a manageable, interpretable partition while limiting the chaining seen with single linkage. The three-group cut is an analytical choice rather than a uniquely correct number of clusters.

Cluster 1 (15 cars) contains small, light, economical cars: about 24.7 mpg, 4.5 cylinders on average (mostly 4-cylinder), 91 hp, weight 2.58 and 132 cu. in. displacement, nearly all with straight engines (vs = 0.93) and a mix of transmissions. Cluster 2 (12 cars) holds the big American V8s: 15.0 mpg, 8 cylinders, 358 cu. in., 194 hp, the heaviest (weight 4.10), slowest quarter-mile times (17.1 s), all automatic with 3 gears. Cluster 3 (5 cars: Mazda RX4/RX4 Wag, Ford Pantera L, Ferrari Dino, Maserati Bora) are lighter performance cars: the highest horsepower (198.8 hp) at a moderate weight (3.00), the fastest quarter-mile (15.6 s), all manual with 4.6 gears on average and many carburetors (5.2), at 18.5 mpg.

Why this choice: In the complete-linkage dendrogram the largest height gap is between the top merge (about 8.5) and the next merges (about 6), which suggests two main groups (15 small cars vs. 17 larger/high-powered cars). I cut further to three clusters because the 17-car group separates cleanly into the heavy V8s and the lighter manual performance cars, which the table of means confirms. The three-cluster cut is therefore a judgment about interpretability, not the only defensible choice (the two-cluster cut has the clearest height gap). Single linkage was not used because it merely peels off one or two outlying cars, and average linkage gives a similar picture but isolates only Ford Pantera L and Maserati Bora in a small third group.

Why standardize? Without scaling, variables with large numerical ranges (for example, horsepower or displacement) can dominate Euclidean distance, even when other variables are just as substantively important. Z-scoring places all 11 variables on comparable unit-variance scales so the clustering reflects their combined standardized differences.
